By John Schultz, CAI, AMM | Grafe Auction Company

*This article describes our experience with Nebraska's auction-house permit process in August and September 2026.*

When a retailer closes, the remaining alcohol inventory can create a question that doesn't come up with shelving or refrigeration: can we include it in the auction? Nebraska now has an auction-house permit for qualifying alcohol sales, and our experience obtaining one offers a place to start.

We encountered that question at Maline's Superfoods in Sutherland while preparing for our September 1, 2026 auction. The seller wanted us to include the remaining packaged alcohol. The law was in place, but the Nebraska Liquor Control Commission was still developing the application process.

Mark Watson and Briana Salgado with the Nebraska Liquor Control Commission (NLCC) worked with us through a temporary administrative process. When Briana sent the permit on August 27, she confirmed that it was the first of its kind issued to an auction house.

## Start with where the alcohol came from

The Legislature created the permit in [LB478, approved April 7, 2025](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/109/PDF/Slip/LB478.pdf). Now found in [Neb. Rev. Stat. § 53-123.19](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=53-123.19), it covers two categories:

- Alcohol previously sold to a retailer that is no longer operating.
- Alcohol in a private collection that was previously sold at retail to a consumer in Nebraska.

Maline's fit the first category. For an estate auctioneer, the second category is worth knowing about. A wine or whiskey collection may qualify, but being part of an estate doesn't establish that it meets the law's conditions. If its purchase history is unclear, ask the Commission what documentation it needs before agreeing to auction it.

The law requires an auction permit, notice to the Commission of the auctions, and inventories of the alcohol to be sold. A store's existing liquor license isn't something the auctioneer can simply take over. Nebraska treats liquor licenses as personal privileges that aren't transferable. [§ 53-149](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=53-149)

## How I'd approach the next sale

I'd start with the [Nebraska Liquor Control Commission](https://lcc.nebraska.gov/) and ask for the current auction-house permit application and instructions. Explain the source of the alcohol, where it is stored, how bidding will work, and the proposed auction and pickup dates. That gives the staff a specific sale to evaluate.

For our sale, the Commission supplied an application, an applicant attestation, and an auction notice and inventory form. We returned the signed application with a seven-page inventory. Here is what I'd have ready for that conversation:

1. An inventory showing lot number, brand or product name, type, container size, quantity, source category, and owner or consignor.
2. A plan for verifying bidders' and purchasers' age and identity, including how remote bidding and pickup will work.
3. Procedures for secure storage, payment, release of the product, and handling anything unsold or unclaimed.

The Commission's instructions required a separate notice and inventory for each auction before alcohol was first offered for bidding. Additions required an amended inventory before those products could be offered. We kept the alcohol off the public lot list and out of advertising while we worked through approval.

Our temporary application stated that no auction-house permit fee was being assessed under that process. I wouldn't assume that fee treatment or the forms will stay the same. Get the current requirements and allow time for review before committing to a sale date.

## Be specific about bidding and pickup

Our submitted plan used online identity verification, followed by an in-person government photo ID check confirming the purchaser was at least 21 and matched the paid account. Only the winning bidder could collect the alcohol, after payment in full. We proposed no shipping or third-party pickup.

We also limited access to the stored product, matched the catalog to the filed inventory, and provided for securing unsold or unclaimed alcohol. Those were the procedures we submitted for our auction. If you want to offer delivery, shipping, or pickup by someone else, have the Commission review that arrangement.

The application made another boundary clear: an auction permit does not itself authorize operating a bar, serving alcohol, or allowing consumption on the premises. That restriction should not be confused with choices we made about pickup.

## Check what the permit actually covers

Our permit arrived August 27, but it became effective August 28 and expired September 5. It authorized online bidding and identified the Sutherland location for storage and pickup. Receiving the document and reaching its effective date were separate steps.

Check the approved dates, location, bidding method, and pickup hours against your actual plans. If a buyer might collect after the permit expires, or you may need to move or offer unsold alcohol again, ask the Commission how to handle that before proceeding. Don't assume the original approval covers the change.

I appreciated Mark and Briana's willingness to work through the details with us. We had a seller with inventory to sell, and their work gave us an approved way to include it. For another auctioneer facing that question, I'd make the call early and have the inventory and proposed sale procedures ready.

---

*Related option for closed retailers: If the seller is considering transferring the remaining stock to another qualified licensee, ask the Commission about the existing stock-transfer procedure. Chapter 2, sections 03.01 and 03.02 of the [NLCC rules and regulations](https://lcc.nebraska.gov/enforcement-citations/rules-and-regulations) address transfers after a business has discontinued operations and its license has terminated. Both parties must apply in writing, and inventory information is required.*


## FAQ

### Do I need a permit to auction alcohol in Nebraska?

Nebraska requires an auction permit for the qualifying sales described in section 53-123.19. Start with the Nebraska Liquor Control Commission and ask for the current application and instructions.

### Can an estate wine or whiskey collection qualify?

It may qualify if the alcohol was previously sold at retail to a consumer in Nebraska. If the purchase history is unclear, ask the Commission what documentation it needs before agreeing to auction it.

### Does this permit allow shipping or third-party pickup?

Our submitted plan included neither shipping nor third-party pickup. If you want either option, have the Commission review the arrangement before offering it.
