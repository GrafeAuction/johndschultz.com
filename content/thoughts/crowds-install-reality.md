## The Observation

I was listening to Lex Fridman with Julia Shaw on the bystander effect, and the piece that stuck was simple: if you're not first on the scene, you look at the other faces to figure out what this is. Calm faces mean "not an emergency." Your calm becomes data for the next person who walks up.

Lex's point was that most people think they'd resist evil, or step up on a street when something bad is happening. Most people don't. You have to simulate the hard act of resisting, or of intervening, because in the moment it's not easy.

Shaw brought in Zimbardo's "heroic imagination." The idea is you study how ordinary people slide into doing harm, then train yourself to catch the slide and stop. She also cleaned up the Kitty Genovese myth. Extreme cases get used as proof that humans don't care. Later bystander research doesn't back that. Most of the time people do intervene. The freeze hits when a crowd has already formed and nobody has moved. You read the room. You assume it must not be a real problem. The desire not to stand out in a negative way is what kills the heroic move.

Dark thought, but it followed: if you wanted a bad act to stay unchallenged, you might load the room with people who look like bystanders. Not because witnesses don't matter. Because the next real person will copy the calm and decide everything is fine. You're not primarily hiding the act. You're hijacking the decision rule.

## The Auction Version

I'm reading Ellen Hartigan-O'Connor's *America Under the Hammer*, and Chapter 1 lands on almost the same mechanism.

She writes that by repeatedly exposing colonial communities to the idea that people could be priced, auctions supported a notion that economic logic determined social relations rather than the other way around. Broad participation mattered. Bidding at an auction validated that the items put up for sale were exchangeable. That opened the door to treating land as a liquid asset rather than an inalienable inheritance or sacred homeland.

She's writing about colonial America, including human beings put under the hammer. That history is its own moral catastrophe. What I'm pulling from the chapter is the mechanism: public pricing can make almost anything feel normal if the room practices it long enough.

I think that's the bystander mechanism wearing an auctioneer's hat.

On the street, the question is: is this a crisis?
In the yard, the question is: is this a commodity?

The crowd answers both. Stillness answers "no, not a crisis." Bidding answers "yes, this can be sold." The sale doesn't only discover a price. The room keeps proving the thing is for sale, over and over, until it feels like nature.

I've watched this in the yard. A thin room doesn't just mean lower prices. It means the category feels soft. The seller can still hear their own story about what the asset is worth. A full room, phones up, bids overlapping, and the same asset starts feeling like inventory. The thing didn't change. The faces around it did.

And, that's a darker thought for my industry than it first sounds. Law can authorize a sale. Ritual makes it feel true. The knock of the hammer is a small referendum the market keeps winning. Some people in that room are eager. Some are just present. Presence still counts, because a full, calm room is the proof that this is ordinary economic life, not a moral emergency.

Shaw wants a heroic imagination: pre-load the self so you don't wait for the crowd's permission to intervene. Hartigan-O'Connor is describing a market imagination: a civic ritual that trains the eye until pricing people and places feels like common sense. Same wiring. Opposite training program.

## What Follows

Defaults work the same way. They don't announce themselves. They accumulate. A bystander default is "wait and see what everyone else does." An auction culture default is "if it's under the hammer, it's property." Both feel like decisions from the outside. Both can be paths nobody consciously chose.

That's also why [true market value](/essays/true-market-value/) is a room problem, not only a marketing problem. Value gets made when people show up and bid, because bidding is the act that says this is exchangeable at a clearing price. Empty room, weak category. Full room, the category hardens. An auction is still the day private opinion gets laid against a public record. Without a crowd, that check never happens. Just private opinion continuing unchallenged.

The first mover is the hinge in both stories. On the street, one person who kneels, points, and assigns roles rewrites the social proof for everyone else. In the yard, the counter-rituals look different: refusal, naming, not bidding. Heroism isn't always diving into the river. Sometimes it's breaking the calm conspiracy before the room finishes defining you.

Which leaves the open question I actually care about as an auctioneer: when the thing in the ring is contested, emotional, or half-sacred to the seller, is the job only to make the market, or also to keep the room from installing the wrong kind of reality too fast?
