## The Observation

I was explaining how I take notes to someone on a call. I didn't start them on my whole digital system. I handed them a pack of habits that fit on an index card.

Front: date, plus the idea that stopped them. Back: source, and why it mattered. One idea only. A highlight, a short conversation snippet, a Bible verse, a few sentences that can stand alone. Not a chapter dump. Not a book summary. Not a sticky note you'll never find again.

The example I used was Costco on the Acquired podcast, a line attributed to a conversation between Jeff Bezos and Jim Sinegal. Filled in the way I'd actually write it:

Front: 2026-07-28. Companies that work hard to charge more vs companies that work hard to charge less.

Back: Acquired podcast, Costco episode (Bezos / Jim Sinegal conversation, as told there). Why it mattered: clean pricing posture I can use when someone confuses "premium brand" with "we add margin because we can." Same fork shows up in auction marketing.

That's an atom. Someone else could take the same episode and write a different one. The unit is what stopped *you*, not a show outline. Without the why on the back, you have a clipping service, not attention training.

I run the same practice on books, podcasts, calls, and Scripture. Capture as near the spark as possible. Mid-chapter is fine. Mid-call, a keyword is fine if writing would break the room; finish the atom the same day. Driving: jot, then clean it up when you're parked. Do not wait until "I finish the book."

I'm living in thought mode. Intentional reading, listening, relationships. Atomic notes are how that posture leaves a trail. Zettelkasten-shaped, not software-demo shaped. The note is not the point. The noticing is.

In 2026 alone I've taken thousands of these. That number is evidence the habit is real. It is not a scoreboard. If I start chasing count, I've already lost the plot.

## The portable core

Gear is simple. Index cards or a small notebook you will keep, and a pen. Optional later: a folder, a notes app, whatever you can search and keep. Sticky notes are out. If you can't find it in a month, it wasn't a system.

If two independent claims show up, that is two cards. A quote alone only counts if the quote *is* the claim you could defend without the source open.

I also ask people to be ruthless about what does *not* get a card. Logistics (times, addresses, "call X Tuesday") go on a calendar or task list. Whole chapter or book dumps stay out. So does "good point" / "I like this" with no portable idea. Don't mint a twin for a claim you already have; add the new source on the back of the first, or link them later. Drop noise from books, podcasts, or follows you've already decided don't enrich you.

There are two clocks, and they do different jobs.

Capture is daily and near-moment: name the idea. That trains attention. I will not throttle this in a busy season. Ups and downs are part of living. Overgating the noticing is how the habit dies.

Review is where interest compounds. Every 7-10 days, 15-20 minutes: finish thin "whys," turn jots into real cards, kill duplicates, stack the week in one place. Once a month, longer: flip older cards and look for the same shape showing up twice. When you can say the relationship in half a sentence ("this is the same idea from Caples," "this challenges what I wrote after that call"), write that connection down. Orphans are fine until the connection is real. In between, if something sparks a collision, follow it. Don't wait for the calendar.

Buy a car and you see that model everywhere. Same effect. The review is how you notice the pattern on purpose.

If you use AI, keep it downstream. After you have real atoms, it can help you search, cluster, or research deeper. Do not start with "summarize this chapter into notes for me." That outsources the noticing. Same fork as sparring versus ghostwriting: tools after judgment, not instead of it.

Week one, if you want a floor: read or listen with a purpose (one chapter a day is enough). Take cards only when something stops you. No quota. End of the first week, one short review pass. End of the first month, one longer pass. Capture alone still trains attention. Both clocks are what make it a Zettelkasten.

My own stack is bigger than what I handed them. Cards, plus searchable digital notes that link to each other, plus AI after the atoms exist. Date-named files, connected ideas, the same trash rules as the rest of my system. That stack is unique to me. Not required to begin. The portable core is the noticing. The stack is how I keep the interest from rotting once the volume gets real.

## When it goes wrong

Scoreboard drift is the cleanest failure. Volume becomes the goal. Review becomes optional. The pile looks impressive and stops paying interest.

There's also the beautiful empty graph: thin "why it mattered," AI drawing edges I never thought, [digital exhaust](/thoughts/digital-exhaust/) that looks like compound interest and isn't.

Sticky-note cosplay is capture without trackability. Feels productive. Can't compound.

Upstream AI is the other fork. Summaries instead of noticing. The habit still produces files. Attention never trained.

I think capture without review is still useful as attention practice. It is not a Zettelkasten. Compounding needs the second clock. If review dies for a long stretch, call that half broken and clear the backlog before bragging about count. Don't throttle the noticing to protect the filing system.

## Open Questions

1. When the cards keep stacking but the monthly pass is thin, am I still training attention, or am I collecting evidence that only looks like a habit?
2. How often is the "why it mattered" true in the moment, and how often do I invent it later so the card feels complete?
3. If the noticing is the point, when does my full digital system stop being the compound layer and start being costume for a practice that already finished at the pen?
