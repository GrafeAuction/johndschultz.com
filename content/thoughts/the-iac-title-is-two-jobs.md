## The Observation

I spent a long day scoring auctioneers. Prelims first. Then finals: interview, then selling again.

Prelims decide who keeps competing. They ask a hard question: can you call bids under pressure, in front of people who do this for a living? That skill is real. It's not the whole title.

The final score is not the prelim run again. You sit and answer for the trade. Then you sell again on the block. Only people who advance face that second test.

The interview was not small talk. Two questions carried real weight.

One asked what auctioneers should do to build trust in a world that doubts people. The auction method runs on people trusting people, and that trust is under strain. What will you do about it?

The other asked, if you became International Auctioneer Champion, how you would use the title to help the auction trade. If we give you the mic, what happens next?

Those aren't chant questions. They describe the job. Bid calling gets you into the finals. The interview asks if you can speak for the trade.

I think those two skills need not rise and fall together. A person can sell well and answer poorly. Another can speak well for the trade and still have to prove it when the bidding starts. The format scores both on purpose.

On my card alone, the pattern was clear. How someone sold and how they answered did not match.

The skill that got people into finals lined up more with final-round selling than with the interview. The interview was not a style score. Most points sat on the two answers: trust, and what you would do with the title. How you looked mattered less than what you said.

That's still only one judge's view that day. The card measures two jobs.

I was one of seven judges. We did not trade marks at the table. No "what did you give them?" Each card is a vote. The champion is seven honest, uneven votes combined. One sure judge is still one slice of the day.

## The Arc

**Who advances.** A championship must cut the field. Prelims do that with the chant, the command, the sale, the skill the crowd came to hear. If you cannot do that under lights, you do not move on. Fair.

**What changes.** Once you advance, a second job comes first. You sit and speak as if the trade is listening, then you stand and sell as if the trade is watching. Same person, two tests, different skills. The final is not the prelim with higher stakes.

**The two questions.** Trust asks how you keep the auction method honest when people are wary. The title question asks what you would do with short-lived public authority. Together they say a champion is more than the best sound in the room. A champion is someone the trade can put on a stage without regret.

**The split.** Skill on the block and skill in the interview are linked, but they're not the same. Train only rhythm and speed and you may sell well and still lose points in the chair. Train only smooth answers and you may talk well and still have to earn it when the bidding starts. The card will not let either half stand for the whole.

**The panel.** Seven judges. Separate marks. No talk at the table. That is how you stop one person's taste from running the contest. My card was my view that day. It was not the contest. My read is that the contest is seven views averaged, including where I was sharper than the room and where the room was sharper than me.

## The Implication

If you hire an auctioneer for a hard sale, you may pick for the block: rhythm, accuracy, control when things get loud. That hire makes sense.

If you name an International Auctioneer Champion, you do something else. You name a short-term public face of the trade. The title travels. It shows up in photos, on stages, and in talk about what the trade stands for. The IAC final is built for that. Trust and the title questions get scores because the title is public, not because they're extras on top of the chant.

So if you're chasing the championship, or building a name past one sale, train for both. The block is where you prove you can sell. The interview is where you prove you know why this way of selling still matters, and what you would do if people listened to you as more than a bid caller. Same test applies when you hire or put someone on a stage for the company or the association: know whether you need best craft, best public face, or both. When the job includes speaking for a group, score the speaking. Don't assume the craft score covers it.

For me, with a laptop and a private card, the design asks for restraint. I don't set the official order from my seat. I put honest numbers on what I see, then six other honest sets of numbers meet mine. That push and pull is the point.

## Open Questions

1. How do competitors train for the interview without turning it into a show? Answers must hold up for seven judges who do not compare notes, not for one friendly listener alone.
2. When we hire, promote, or put someone on a stage for the company or the association, do we know which job we are filling: best craft, best public face, or both? The IAC forces both. Most groups blur it.
3. Should the trade try to close the gap between block skill and interview skill with better training, or is the gap useful news that a good contest is meant to show?
